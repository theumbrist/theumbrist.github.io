PANAMA vs HONG KONG

Panama Files Criminal Complaint Against CK Hutchison's Canal Port Unit

Panama's state auditor took a decade-long audit of Panama Ports Company to prosecutors on November 18. The move lands while CK Hutchison pursues more than $1.5bn in treaty arbitration against the state.

THE BRIEFING · FULL TEXT

Panama's Comptroller General's Office filed a criminal complaint with the Public Prosecutor's Office on November 18 over suspected irregularities in an audit of Panama Ports Company, the CK Hutchison subsidiary that ran the container terminals at Balboa and Cristóbal.GCAPTAIN (REUTERS), THE MARITIME EXECUTIVE

The audit covered ten years of the company's compliance with its concession contract, examining financial statements and contractual obligations. Reporting on the filing says the complaint also names former officials of the Panama Maritime Authority who served between 2014 and 2023.THE MARITIME EXECUTIVE, GCAPTAIN (REUTERS)

The complaint is the latest step in a dispute that has already changed who controls two of the busiest terminals on the interoceanic route. In January 2026, Panama's Supreme Court ruled the legal framework behind the concession unconstitutional. The state then took control of the facilities.GCAPTAIN (REUTERS), AL JAZEERA

CK Hutchison's unit had operated Balboa on the Pacific side and Cristóbal on the Atlantic side since the 1990s. The case that killed the concession argued that the underlying laws were unconstitutional and that the operator had not paid what it owed. After the ruling, Panama's government brought in Maersk to manage the terminals on a temporary basis until a new concession is awarded.AL JAZEERA

In August 2026, CK Hutchison started international investment-treaty arbitration against Panama, seeking more than $1.5bn in damages over the takeover of its port investments. That claim is the pressure the complaint now answers.GCAPTAIN (REUTERS)

The wider frame is a contest between Washington and Beijing over the canal. US President Donald Trump threatened in late 2024 to take control of the waterway, saying it was effectively run by China. CK Hutchison then put dozens of terminals worldwide, including the two Panamanian ones, up for sale to a consortium led by BlackRock in a deal valued at close to $23bn.AL JAZEERA

Beijing did not treat the court ruling as a domestic legal matter. China's Hong Kong and Macao Affairs Office called the decision absurd and shameful and said Panama would pay heavy prices politically and economically. President José Raúl Mulino rejected the threat and said Panama respects the decisions of an independent judiciary.AL JAZEERA

The gains from the complaint sit with the Panamanian state. It holds the terminals, it controls the audit file, and it now has a prosecutor's process running in parallel with an arbitration it did not choose. A criminal track inside Panama is one thing a foreign tribunal cannot take away.GCAPTAIN (REUTERS), THE MARITIME EXECUTIVE

One reading is that the complaint shifts the subject of the dispute. CK Hutchison wants the tribunal to look at state conduct: a court ruling, a seizure, a destroyed investment. A prosecutor's file lets Panama argue about the contract itself and about how it was granted and extended. This is interpretation, not a finding of any tribunal.

The losses sit with CK Hutchison and with the buyers of its global ports portfolio. The company's cash flow from Balboa and Cristóbal is gone, and the legal cloud over the Panamanian assets has complicated the sale of the wider terminal business to the BlackRock-led group. Each new proceeding adds another year of uncertainty to assets that were supposed to be sold.AL JAZEERA, GCAPTAIN (REUTERS)

Maersk is the quiet beneficiary of the sequence so far. It runs the terminals on an interim basis while the legal questions are litigated elsewhere. The longer the arbitration runs, the more normal that arrangement looks to shippers who only care that boxes move.AL JAZEERA

What breaks next depends on the prosecutors. If the Public Prosecutor's Office opens a formal investigation and charges individuals, the dispute stops being purely commercial and starts touching named people, including former regulators. If it quietly declines, the complaint becomes a bargaining chip with a short shelf life.GCAPTAIN (REUTERS), THE MARITIME EXECUTIVE

The second variable is the concession itself. Panama has said the terminals will go to a new operator. The terms of that tender, and who wins it, will tell more about the country's alignment than any statement from the presidency. A US or European winner locks in the outcome Washington wanted without a single American vessel moving.AL JAZEERA

Signals to watch: whether prosecutors confirm an investigation and name defendants; whether CK Hutchison amends its claim to cover the criminal filing as further state pressure; the schedule and seat of the arbitration; progress or collapse of the sale of the global ports portfolio; and the timing of Panama's tender for a permanent operator at Balboa and Cristóbal.GCAPTAIN (REUTERS), THE MARITIME EXECUTIVE

US + DENMARK

Denmark ratifies US-Greenland pact 109-0, clearing two new Arctic bases

The Folketing approved the September deal without a single vote against. It survives Greenlandic independence and locks non-NATO, non-EU capital out of the island's strategic sectors.

THE BRIEFING · FULL TEXT

Denmark's parliament ratified the security agreement on Greenland with the United States on 19 November. Of the 179 seats in the Folketing, 109 members were present. All 109 voted in favour. There were no votes against and no abstentions, and the text passed its second reading without debate.AL JAZEERA, DEFENSE NEWS

The agreement was signed in New York on 22 September by Donald Trump, Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen and Greenland's Premier Jens-Frederik Nielsen, on the margins of the UN General Assembly. It supplements the 1951 defence accord that governs the American military presence on the island rather than replacing it.DEFENSE NEWS, AL JAZEERA

Three clauses carry the weight. The United States may build two new military bases. The agreement stays in force if Greenland becomes an independent state. And countries outside NATO and the European Union are shut out of Greenland's strategic sectors, a provision aimed at Chinese and Russian investment.DEFENSE NEWS

The two new defence areas are named: Narsarsuaq in the south and Mestersvig in the northeast. Narsarsuaq held the American Bluie West One base until 1958 and now has a few dozen residents. Mestersvig is a 1960s outpost used by Denmark's Sirius dog sled patrol, maintained by three soldiers, with barracks for 150. The deal also lets Washington modernise and expand Pituffik Space Base in the northwest.AL JAZEERA, DEFENSE NEWS

Greenland's own legislature, the Inatsisartut, went first. On 3 November it approved the agreement with 16 votes in favour, none against and seven abstentions. The Copenhagen vote completed the legislative chain. The agreement enters into force once Denmark's foreign ministry sends Washington a diplomatic note confirming that domestic procedures are finished.AL JAZEERA, DEFENSE NEWS

The pact closes two years of pressure. Trump had pushed openly for control of the island, and before the signing he described the arrangement on Truth Social as an "Infinite Life" agreement, saying the United States would "FOREVER have the complete ability to do what is necessary in Greenland". A State Department official said at the time that the deal lets the United States build further military sites without Greenlandic or Danish approval, and that it would hold even after independence.AL JAZEERA

Copenhagen framed the same text differently. Frederiksen said the agreement recognises the sovereignty and territorial integrity of the Kingdom and the right of the Greenlandic people to self-determination. Danish officials stressed that sovereignty stays with Denmark and that ratification in both parliaments was required.AL JAZEERA

The strategic logic is geography. The shortest air route between Europe and North America runs over Greenland, and the island anchors part of the GIUK gap, where NATO tracks Russian naval movement into the North Atlantic. The agreement also makes room for Trump's planned Golden Dome missile defence system, which needs northern sensor and interceptor sites.AL JAZEERA

Washington gains the most durable item: basing rights that do not depend on who governs Nuuk. Denmark gains an exit from an annexation dispute with its main ally, which is why the vote drew no opposition. Beijing and Moscow lose a commercial route into Arctic ports, mining and infrastructure, since the exclusion clause bars them from strategic sectors regardless of future Greenlandic policy.DEFENSE NEWS, AL JAZEERA

Greenland's position is thinner. Naaja Nathanielsen, a Greenlandic member of the Danish parliament, said American pressure had bred distrust of the Trump administration, that the past two years had been difficult for Greenlanders and that the pressure had seeped into every part of their lives. She said she hoped the agreement would reopen dialogue. The seven abstentions in Nuuk sit alongside that assessment.DEFENSE NEWS, AL JAZEERA

One reading is that the sequence matters more than the vote count. The security terms were fixed before the independence question was settled, which narrows what a future independent Greenland could renegotiate about foreign forces on its territory. That is interpretation, not a claim in the text.

The resource question is already contested. On 14 October Trump said massive oil, gas and mineral exploration would begin in Greenland, claiming the island holds some of the world's most easily extractable oil reserves. Denmark's foreign ministry replied that the September agreement changed no extraction rules and granted no mining or drilling rights, and demanded that any exploration respect Greenlandic law. Greenland banned uranium mining and future oil exploration in 2021.AL JAZEERA

What breaks next sits in the implementation detail. The agreement says the two new defence areas will be set up according to modalities and technical details the parties agree later, and it leaves room for additional defence areas beyond the two named sites. Those follow-on talks, not the ratified text, will decide troop numbers, construction timelines and how far American activity extends.AL JAZEERA, DEFENSE NEWS

Signals to watch: the date of the Danish diplomatic note that brings the pact into force; the first construction or survey contracts at Narsarsuaq and Mestersvig; any American request for a third defence area; how the exclusion clause is applied to specific investment cases in mining and ports; and whether Greenlandic parties that abstained in November turn the basing terms into an election issue.DEFENSE NEWS, AL JAZEERA

  • Non-NATO investment barred in Greenland

US vs CHINA

Kenya Picks US Over China for $60bn Rare-Earth Hill

Kenya just handed a $60 billion rare-earth and niobium deposit to a US-led consortium, rejecting Chinese and Australian rivals — but the real prize was never the ore. Tonight's briefing breaks down why the refining stage, not the mining con

THE BRIEFING · FULL TEXT

China digs roughly seventy percent of the world's rare earths. It refines about ninety percent of them. Last week Kenya handed a hill worth sixty billion dollars to an American consortium, over Chinese bidders. The contest begins after the ore leaves the ground.

Briefing number twelve. Tonight's file: how a mining tender in southeastern Kenya became a proxy contest over the next decade of rare-earth supply. By the end of this you'll understand why the ore was never the prize.

First, the move itself. Kenya's Ministry of Mining has named the winner of the tender to develop Mrima Hill. The award went to a group called the Frontier Minerals consortium: three American firms, ReElement Technologies, American Resources Corporation, and Bunker Hill Silver Corp. Seven other bidders lost, among them Chinese and Australian companies, according to the BBC.

Mrima Hill sits in Kwale County, in Kenya's southeast, inland from the Indian Ocean coast. It is a hill, not a mountain range. Underneath it, the ministry estimates critical minerals worth more than sixty billion dollars, mostly rare earths and niobium. Niobium is the quieter of the two. It goes into high-strength steel alloys, into superconductors, into defence-grade magnets.

The ministry ran it as a competitive tender, and Chinese companies were among the bidders that lost, according to the BBC. On the other side of the table, American officials had already flagged this specific hill. US officials have named Mrima Hill a key target in the Trump administration's drive to widen American access to critical minerals, a sector whose processing China currently dominates. So the tender was never a quiet procurement exercise. Both capitals knew the file by name.

Now, the view from Washington. Start with an uncomfortable fact about this industry. Mining concessions are not scarce. Rare earths are, despite the name, reasonably widespread in the earth's crust. What is scarce is the plant that takes mixed ore and separates it into single high-purity oxides. That is where China's position actually sits. It mines around seventy percent of global raw output, but it holds roughly ninety percent of processing capacity and about ninety-nine percent of heavy rare-earth separation, by the Center for Strategic and International Studies' accounting. The narrow point sits in the middle of the chain, not at the pit.

Which is why the identity of the winning consortium matters more than the size of the deposit. And the deposit is large. The ministry puts the minerals under that hill above sixty billion dollars, mostly rare earths and niobium. Tonnage and grade figures do circulate, in Kenyan trade press and open-source compilations. There is no government-audited reserve statement to check them against, so we will leave them out.

Here is the pairing. The group that won the rock is not a mining house alone. ReElement Technologies sits inside it, alongside American Resources. Its stated business is the separation step, the part China holds. Figures for its refinery projects have appeared only in trade and investor-facing outlets, so we are not going to put numbers on them here.

One reading is that this is a corridor being assembled in public, mine at one end, separation plants at the other, with no Chinese midstream step between them. The timing fits a clock. Beijing imposed export licensing on seven rare earths and magnets in April last year, widened it in October, then suspended the second wave on the seventh of November until the tenth of November 2026. That suspension window closed nine days before Kenya announced its winner. Read that sequence how you like. The deadline was on every negotiator's calendar.

Now, Nairobi's side of the table. Kenya was not the weaker party here. A government sitting on one of the world's larger niobium and rare-earth deposits controls a thing that cannot be built, only found. And it chose to stage the decision as a competition rather than a handshake, with seven losing bidders, Chinese companies among them, on the record. That framing does work. It converts a geopolitical choice into an administrative outcome.

The commercial logic is narrower than the headlines suggest. For Nairobi, the question is not which superpower it prefers. It's which bidder's capital, timetable and offtake terms actually move sixty billion dollars of rock into revenue, and how fast. Belt-and-Road financing has historically won African tenders on speed. A consortium that owns its own separation capacity can offer something different: a buyer at the far end of the chain who is not also a competitor.

Now the demand side, in numbers. The European Parliament's research service puts EU imports from China at around sixty percent of its heavy rare earths and eighty-five percent of its light rare earths. And for finished magnets, ninety-eight percent. On magnets, Europe has effectively one supplier. Any non-Chinese deposit of real scale is therefore priced on politics as much as on geology.

The exposure runs the other way too. One interpretation, and it is interpretation, is that Nairobi has traded one dependency for a different kind of risk. Separation plants take years to commission, and ore earns nothing until one exists to take it. A producer with a dominant market share can lower prices faster than a rival can finish a refinery. That tactic has a history in this industry. Kenya's award buys a partner. Whether it buys a functioning corridor is a question for the construction schedule.

We have seen a version of this before, though the weapons were different. May 1978, the southern edge of what was then Zaire. Katangese separatist fighters of the FNLC crossed into Shaba province and took the mining town of Kolwezi. Kolwezi sat on something like half to two-thirds of world cobalt supply, and cobalt in 1978 meant jet engines and missile guidance. The rebels were reportedly backed from Angola and the Soviet bloc, though that backing has always been argued over rather than proven.

The supply effect was immediate. A large share of world cobalt output stopped moving for months, and prices roughly tripled. French and Belgian troops were airlifted in to retake the town and evacuate European residents. Afterwards, Western governments did the predictable thing: they widened their sourcing and built strategic stockpiles, so that no single contested African province could do that to them twice. The Washington Post reported at the time that Soviet buyers had accumulated cobalt before the invasion. Reported, not established.

Now the divergence, and it is the whole point. Shaba was a kinetic shock over a mine the West already held, resolved by paratroopers inside a fortnight. Mrima Hill is the mirror image. A peacetime tender, run by a sovereign African government, decided by a ministry weighing competing offers of capital and processing capacity. Nobody landed troops. The instruments were financing terms and refinery blueprints. What has stayed constant across forty-eight years is the shape of the problem: a single province holding a disproportionate share of one input, and outside powers deciding they cannot live with the other side controlling it.

Two signals from the noise this week. First, Greenland. Denmark's parliament ratified its security agreement with Washington and Nuuk on second reading. All one hundred and nine members present voted in favour. None voted against, in a chamber of one hundred and seventy-nine seats. The pact, signed in New York on the twenty-second of September, permits two new American military bases and supplements the 1951 defence agreement. It bars countries outside the EU and NATO from strategic sectors on the island, aimed at Chinese and Russian investment. And it survives Greenlandic independence, should that come. Greenland's own parliament approved it in early November, sixteen votes in favour with seven abstentions.

Second, Panama. The Comptroller General's Office filed a criminal complaint with prosecutors on the eighteenth of November over suspected irregularities in an audit of Panama Ports Company, a CK Hutchison unit, Reuters reported. In January, Panama's Supreme Court found the concession's legal framework unconstitutional and the state took the facilities. In August, CK Hutchison filed investment-treaty arbitration seeking more than one and a half billion dollars. Two chokepoints, one week, the same question of who may own the infrastructure.

So here is the question to sit with. Washington out-tendered Beijing once, in one African country, against a deadline that Beijing itself had set with its export-licence suspension. The chess question is whether that was a repeatable method or a single favourable alignment of clocks. Ask it the other way as well. So if separation and refining capacity are the real constraint, what does owning the rock at Mrima Hill actually buy — beyond deciding where the next bottleneck gets poured in concrete, and who holds the keys to that building when it opens.

  • China refines 90 percent globally

US vs VENEZUELA

Hegseth Signs Military Pact in Caracas, Ten Months After Maduro's Capture

The US defense secretary flew to Caracas unannounced and signed a deal restoring full military cooperation with Venezuela's interim government. The security track now runs parallel to a 17-field oil program.

THE BRIEFING · FULL TEXT

US Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth landed in Caracas on November 18, 2026, on a trip the Pentagon had not announced, and signed an agreement to reestablish full military ties between the United States and Venezuela. The text covers joint training, intelligence sharing, equipment cooperation and coordination on border and counter-narcotics security. He presented it alongside interim President Delcy Rodriguez at the presidential palace.AP, REUTERS

Hegseth described the shift in blunt terms. Venezuela, he said, had gone from "our most aggressive regional threat to a compliant security partner" now rebuilding its military alongside the United States. He added a line aimed at trafficking networks: "To the narco terrorists who have used this as a safe haven, your time is up." The stated joint objective is to stabilize the country and remove what he called narco-terrorists.AL JAZEERA, REUTERS

The visit was the first by the Pentagon chief since January, when US special operations forces removed the government that had run Venezuela for more than a decade. Operation Absolute Resolve, launched on January 3, 2026, used Delta Force and FBI units to capture President Nicolas Maduro and his wife in Caracas. US officials assessed that roughly 75 people were killed in the raid.WASHINGTON POST, REUTERS

Two days later, on January 5, Vice President Delcy Rodriguez was sworn in as interim president. By the end of that month, Venezuela's police and armed forces had pledged loyalty to her. The military deal signed in November formalizes what has been an improvised relationship since then: an American-installed administration dealing with the force that installed it.CNN, AL JAZEERA

The second track is oil. Hegseth said Washington wants to unlock Venezuela's "incredible resource capabilities," a reference to the largest proven crude reserves in the world. Rodriguez has outlined a 25-year bilateral oil project covering 17 strategic fields with a target above 1.5 million barrels per day. In October she put expected investment at close to $76 billion over five years, with plans to deploy 250 rigs, as part of a package of 61 signed agreements.THE HILL, AL JAZEERA

Those numbers sit against a modest baseline. Venezuelan output reached about 1.2 million barrels per day in October 2026, a seven-year high, and PDVSA's own target for the year was 1.3 million. Chevron produces roughly 244,000 barrels per day through three joint ventures with the state company. Analysts quoted this year see production near 1.5 million by 2028 and consider anything above 2 million unlikely without deep structural reform and sustained foreign capital.REUTERS, CNBC

One reading is that the security agreement is the enabling layer for the oil program rather than a separate policy. Fields, pipelines, ports and rig crews need protection, and a 25-year horizon needs a guarantor that outlasts any single Venezuelan cabinet. This is interpretation, not a claim made by either government.

The gainers are visible. Rodriguez converts American protection into legitimacy and revenue, having already been removed from the US sanctions list in April. The Pentagon acquires training access, intelligence feeds and basing-adjacent cooperation in a country it treated as hostile a year ago. US and allied investors get entry into reserves that were effectively closed, at a moment when capital is also moving into Argentina's Vaca Muerta and Guyana's offshore blocks. Trump described Venezuela this week as an "acquisition."AL JAZEERA, OILPRICE

The losers are the actors with no seat at the table. María Corina Machado, the opposition leader and Nobel Peace Prize winner, returned from more than ten months in exile in late October and has pressed for elections next year. She has been largely absent from the talks that produced the investment and security deals, and many politicians remain barred from standing. Rodriguez told the UN General Assembly in September that elections would come as part of a transition to "full democracy" without naming a date.NPR, REUTERS

Also exposed are the armed groups the agreement targets. Joint operations with US intelligence support change the risk calculus for trafficking networks on the Colombian border and in the Caribbean, where American naval forces have been active for more than a year. Escalation there is the most likely source of the first casualties under the new framework.REUTERS, AL JAZEERA

Three things can break. The first is sequencing: investment commitments of $76 billion and 250 rigs require years of legal certainty, while an interim government with no electoral mandate can be unwound by a vote. The second is the counter-narcotics mission, which puts US personnel into Venezuelan operations without the legal scaffolding of a treaty ratified by either legislature. The third is control of production. Whoever sets output schedules across 17 fields holds a lever over prices, OPEC discipline and Venezuelan public finances at once.REUTERS

The signals worth tracking are narrow and checkable. Whether the agreement is submitted to the US Congress or to Venezuela's National Assembly in any form. Whether a date for elections appears, and whether Machado's ban is lifted. Whether monthly production figures move toward the 1.3 million target or stall near current levels. Whether US troops appear on Venezuelan soil in training or operational roles, and in what numbers. And whether the first joint counter-narcotics operations are announced with names, locations and results, or remain unattributed.REUTERS, AL JAZEERA

Ten months is a short interval between a raid that killed about 75 people and a signed partnership with the government that followed it. The military text gives that sequence an institutional form. The oil program gives it a timeline of 25 years.WASHINGTON POST, AL JAZEERA

MOZAMBIQUE vs US

Mozambique Clears Exxon's $30bn Rovuma LNG Plan at 18.6 Million Tonnes

Mozambique's cabinet approved ExxonMobil's redesigned Rovuma plant on November 17, ahead of a final investment decision the company wants before year-end. The tonnes land in a market short of Gulf supply.

THE BRIEFING · FULL TEXT

Mozambique's Council of Ministers approved ExxonMobil's amended development plan for the Rovuma LNG project on November 17, 2026. The approval removes the main regulatory obstacle before a final investment decision that the operator expects by the end of this year. The plant will sit at Afungi, in Cabo Delgado province, on the country's northern coast.OILPRICE (REUTERS-SOURCED), LNG PRIME

The amended plan lifts onshore liquefaction capacity to 18.6 million tonnes a year, up from the 15.2 million tonnes approved in 2019. Standard Bank puts the cost at close to $30 billion, which would make Rovuma the largest onshore development in Mozambique's history. Mozambique's National Petroleum Institute estimates state receipts of around $121 billion across a 25-year production life.OILPRICE (REUTERS-SOURCED), LNG PRIME

The redesign is not only bigger. Gas-processing modules will run on electric drive, which the operator says cuts construction cost and lowers emissions by roughly 40 percent against the 2019 design. A quarter of the gas produced is reserved for the domestic market. First output is indicated for the early 2030s, with commercial operations cited from 2031.CONSTRUCTION REVIEW, LNG PRIME

Ownership shifted shortly before the approval. In October 2026 ExxonMobil doubled its working interest in Rovuma to 50 percent through an asset swap with Eni, which took 50 percent of the Coral Sul and Coral Norte floating units and of future floating projects in Area 4. Terms were not disclosed. Exxon stays operator. About $1.1 billion has already been committed to long-lead equipment.BLOOMBERG, CONSTRUCTION REVIEW

The timing matters more than the tonnage. The de facto closure of the Strait of Hormuz at the start of March 2026 cut LNG shipments from Qatar and the United Arab Emirates by more than 300 million cubic metres a day, removing close to a fifth of global supply. QatarEnergy announced a full production shutdown after Iranian strikes on energy installations. Qatar shipped nothing through the strait in August and 1.8 million tonnes in October.IEA, AL JAZEERA

The IEA puts the damage at nearly 70 billion cubic metres of lost Qatari output by 2030 on a four-year repair assumption, and a cumulative supply loss of about 140 billion cubic metres between 2026 and 2030. European TTF spot prices averaged close to $20 per million British thermal units from March to October, almost 70 percent above a year earlier. Asian spot cargoes carried a premium of about $1.50 over European hubs through the middle quarters.IEA

Europe's own rules tighten the squeeze. The EU adopted its regulation phasing out Russian pipeline gas and LNG on January 26, 2026. The stepwise ban began applying from March 18. Long-term Russian LNG contracts fall under the prohibition from January 1, 2027. Europe is therefore removing one supplier by law while a second is offline by force.CONSILIUM (COUNCIL OF THE EU)

Asian demand has been the softer side. Chinese LNG imports fell by 10 percent, or 7 billion cubic metres, in the first ten months of 2026, helped by stronger domestic production. Chinese offtakers resold a record volume of cargoes over the winter period. One reading is that China has become the market's shock absorber rather than its growth engine, which makes European buyers the more likely first signatories on Rovuma volumes. That is interpretation, not a reported fact.IEA

Mozambique gains the most in nominal terms and carries the most risk. The World Bank records a 0.2 percent contraction in 2025 and projects 0.6 percent growth in 2026 as LNG construction resumes, with the gains concentrated in capital-intensive work that creates few jobs. IMF analysis shows LNG project loans rising from $4.6 billion, or 19.6 percent of GDP in 2024, toward $20 billion by 2045, and domestic debt reaching roughly 63 percent of GDP by 2030.WORLD BANK, IMF

Exxon gains a large non-Gulf, non-Russian position at a moment when scarcity rents are high and the cost of the redesign is lower than the 2019 version. Eni gains speed, trading onshore exposure for floating units that reach market faster. Qatar loses the near-monopoly it held on cheap incremental tonnes. Russian exporters lose their last contractual foothold in Europe.BLOOMBERG, IEA

The security file has not closed. Violence reached all 17 districts of Cabo Delgado for the first time this year. At least 28,163 people were displaced between January and July 2026, 65 percent of them women and children. More than 7,000 were displaced since late October, and the province now holds over 205,000 people across more than 80 sites. Total displacement across the conflict exceeds 1.3 million.AL JAZEERA, UN NEWS

TotalEnergies offers the template and the warning. Its neighbouring project declared force majeure in April 2021 after the attack on Palma, lifted it in November 2025, and restarted construction on January 29, 2026, with Rwandan forces deployed under an agreement signed in August 2025. Five years were lost on a site that was already around 40 percent complete.AL JAZEERA, IEA

Four signals decide whether Rovuma becomes steel. Whether the final investment decision actually lands before December 31. Which buyers sign the long-term offtake and on what index, since Europe is contracting under a legal deadline and Asia is reselling. Whether export credit agencies and commercial lenders price Cabo Delgado risk at a level the sponsors accept. And whether Qatari repairs come in at four years or longer, because the whole $30 billion case rests on tonnes arriving in the early 2030s into a market that may by then be amply supplied.IEA, OILPRICE (REUTERS-SOURCED)

US vs CHINA

TSMC and Samsung Suspend China Chip Shipments as US Rule Bites

TSMC and Samsung have suspended certain advanced chip shipments to Chinese customers while applying for new US export licences, after a Nov 16 Commerce Department rule extended licence requirements to any data center worldwide that could ro

THE BRIEFING · FULL TEXT

Two of the world's most advanced chipmakers stopped loading cargo this week. Nobody ordered them to. They did it to stay on the right side of a rule written in Washington, and they are now queuing for American licences before they can resume.

Briefing number eight. Tonight's file: how a rule about data centres became a shipment freeze inside the world's two most advanced chip exporters, three days before a summit in Shenzhen. By the end, you'll understand whether Washington is buying time, or spending a card at the table.

First, the move itself. TSMC in Taiwan and Samsung Electronics in South Korea have suspended shipments of certain advanced chips to customers in China. The Financial Times reports they are applying for new American export licences while the freeze holds. Two companies in two jurisdictions. Neither is American.

The trigger is dated the sixteenth of November. Axios reported that the administration moved that day to close what the industry had been calling the third-party data-centre loophole. The mechanism matters more than the label. Until now, controls followed the hardware. A chip could sit legally in a server hall in a third country, while a customer in China rented the compute remotely, by the hour, over a cable.

So the rule follows the access instead. Axios reported that data centres outside China which sell large-scale compute to Chinese customers now need an American licence to keep buying US-designed chips. No chip has to cross a border for the rule to bite. The customer only has to be able to log in.

Then there is the queue. A shipment that cannot go now does not simply wait. It becomes a licence application, in the same pile as everyone else's, with no published decision time. Both companies chose to stop loading rather than argue the edge cases. Freight is cheap to delay. Export permissions are not cheap to lose. A container leaving Kaohsiung or Busan for a mainland port is a matter of days, which is why the call had to be made this week rather than next.

Now, from Washington. The usual way to describe this is containment. One reading is that it's closer to a stopwatch. China's integrated-circuit industry posted record revenue last year, according to figures reported by CNBC. Around two hundred and forty-five billion dollars. Up more than a fifth on the year. CXMT, which makes memory, more than doubled its revenue.

That changes what a restriction is for. If the gap were stable, controls would be a wall. If the gap is closing, controls are a delay, and the value of a delay depends entirely on what you do with it. SMIC's capacity for wafers below seven nanometres is projected to nearly double by 2027. So the planning horizon here is short. Two years, not twenty.

Then there's the question of who does the enforcing. TSMC and Samsung are not merely restricted by this rule. They're executing it, holding their own cargo and queuing for American licences. That converts the highest-value export sector in Taipei and Seoul into part of the control architecture. Their revenue now moves at the speed of a licensing office in Washington. One interpretation is that this makes hedging toward the Chinese market structurally harder, independent of what either government says in public.

The geography widens too. The licence requirement now reaches server halls in countries that are not China at all. Operators of third-country data centres selling large-scale compute to Chinese customers have ninety days to obtain a licence, or lose their access to US-designed chips, Axios reported. Ninety days. That is the whole negotiation for an industry built on five-year power contracts. Take a commercial data centre in Southeast Asia. The operator of the hall does the filing. Not the chip designer, not the customer renting the racks. And filing means telling a foreign regulator who those customers are, at what scale they buy compute, and whether any of it can be reached from inside China. For a commercial landlord that is an unusual thing to disclose.

Then consider the other branch. A hall that does not file, or files and is refused, keeps its building, its power contract and its cooling. It loses the only chips worth putting inside. Concrete and switchgear are not the scarce part.

Now, look at it from Beijing. The striking thing is how little of this is new information in Beijing. Chinese buyers have spent years working on the assumption that the licence tap could close, and ordering domestic parts accordingly. The record revenue posted by China's chipmakers last year, reported by CNBC, is the receipt. None of that waited for the sixteenth of November.

Which raises an awkward question about who this actually binds. Chinese demand for compute does not disappear when an American licence is refused. It moves down the quality curve and into domestic supply, which is the one outcome no control list can license away. One reading is that the sharper effect falls on the countries in the middle, which now have to prove a negative about their own customers, rather than on Huawei's order book.

Beijing's constraint is not demand. It's the upstream layer. The tools, and the memory that feeds the accelerators. That is where a control regime can still do damage. Toolmakers are the slow part of any chip stack. You can design around a processor in eighteen months. A deposition tool takes longer.

And then the calendar. Bloomberg reports the new curbs force Xi Jinping to weigh a retaliatory response ahead of this week's meeting with Donald Trump. The two men held talks in Shenzhen on the eighteenth and nineteenth, at the APEC summit, both sides looking to extend a trade truce past January 2027. The rule's effective date sits inside that meeting. Rare earths remain Beijing's most immediate instrument of reply.

We have seen a version of this before. From 1949 until 1994, the United States and its allies ran a shared embargo list through a body called CoCom. The Coordinating Committee for Multilateral Export Controls. Its focus narrowed over time onto computing and electronics, aimed at slowing Soviet military-industrial development. Governments negotiated the lists. Customs officers enforced them at the dock.

The outcome is instructive rather than flattering. Estimates of what CoCom achieved vary, and none of them is settled. The range usually cited runs from two to five years of delay to Soviet electronics. Enforcement was uneven. Goods moved through third countries anyway, re-exported by intermediaries, and Moscow eventually acquired much of what the lists were written to withhold. The leak that embarrassed the system most surfaced in 1987. Toshiba Machine, a Japanese toolmaker, had sold precision milling machines to the Soviet Union through a Norwegian partner, Kongsberg. The machines could cut quieter submarine propellers. Two Toshiba Machine executives were arrested in Tokyo, the parent company's chairman resigned, and Washington spent months threatening sanctions against a treaty ally rather than against Moscow. One list, one allied subcontractor, one shipping route. The regime bought years. It did not buy permanence.

Three differences are worth holding onto. CoCom was multilateral, built on agreement between allied capitals; this week's measure is a single national rule, issued without a comparable framework. CoCom controlled physical objects crossing borders; this controls a service. Remote access to compute, which has no customs declaration. And the enforcing is done by companies now. TSMC and Samsung are the ones holding their own cargo and filing the applications, in Hsinchu and in Seoul. That is a different kind of chokepoint. It sits inside a corporate compliance department, not at a port.

One signal from the noise this week. Just the one. Caracas. The AP reports that Defence Secretary Pete Hegseth announced a signed agreement reestablishing military ties with Venezuela, arriving in the capital unannounced on the eighteenth. Hegseth described the country as having moved from a regional threat to a compliant security partner. He cited joint training, intelligence sharing and equipment cooperation. Set that against January. In January, American Delta Force and FBI units captured President Nicolas Maduro and his wife. US officials assessed that about seventy-five people were killed in the raid. Al Jazeera reports that Delcy Rodriguez has since outlined a twenty-five-year bilateral oil project covering seventeen fields, aiming at more than one and a half million barrels a day.

Which brings us back to the licensing queue. Here is the question to hold. If China's own chip industry was already growing at record pace before this rule existed, does closing the data-centre loophole slow the clock, or tighten the incentive to finish the domestic stack? And consider the timing once more. The rule took effect on the second day of the Shenzhen talks. A measure that takes effect mid-summit can be enforced, or it can be traded. The ninety-day deadline is the first place that answer shows up.

YEMEN + IRAN

Why Tanker Rates Crashed While Hormuz Attacks Continued

In the same 72 hours, Yemeni government forces quietly ceded the Red Sea coast near Bab el-Mandeb while two tankers burned in the Strait of Hormuz — two of the world's narrowest shipping arteries under pressure at once. This briefing lays o

THE BRIEFING · FULL TEXT

Supertanker charter rates have fallen more than sixty percent from their record highs since the start of November. That was the same week two tankers caught fire in the Strait of Hormuz. Two readings of the same risk. One of them is wrong.

Briefing number one. Tonight's file: a Yemeni retreat on the Red Sea coast, a wave of tanker attacks in the Gulf, and two of the world's narrowest shipping corridors under pressure in the same seventy-two hours. By the end, you'll understand why holding a chokepoint can be worth more than closing it, and why the freight market is betting none of this bites.

First, the move itself. Yemeni government forces pulled out of Dhubab airport and the ground near Bab el-Mandeb. They redeployed to al-Suqyah, roughly ninety-five miles west of Aden, to defend the capital. There was no battle for the coast. They went somewhere else.

That withdrawal hands the Houthi movement greater leverage over Bab el-Mandeb, the southern gate of the Red Sea. Picture a funnel. At its narrowest, the channel is tighter than the Strait of Dover, and every ship bound for Suez from the Indian Ocean has to thread it. Whoever holds the Yemeni shore holds the approach. The approach is the asset.

So what does holding that shore actually buy? The lane runs close enough to the Yemeni beach that a ship inside it stays within reach of whoever is standing on the sand. The troops who held Dhubab are now dug in inland, facing the defence of the capital instead. Nobody has taken their place on the coast. The Houthi movement inherited the ground without firing.

The Gulf now. Some fourteen hundred miles east, as the crow flies. On Monday the United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations centre reported two tankers attacked in the Strait of Hormuz. Both took engine-room fires. One was left disabled. The same day, a commercial tanker was approached far out in the Arabian Sea by a skiff carrying four armed men. That is open water, well beyond the strait.

And then, ashore. In a formal contracting notice this month, the US Army wrote that soldiers in the region are housed in shelters made from converted shipping containers and, in its words, currently at risk. The same notice orders urgent construction of concrete bunkers across the Middle East. Container walls keep out the sun. They don't keep out a drone.

So: a coastline changing hands without a shot, fires in two engine rooms, and an army going underground. Each one, on its own, is an ordinary week in this part of the world. Together they describe something narrower. Two arteries, squeezed at once.

Now, the view from Tehran. Start with the volume. In normal conditions, about twenty million barrels a day of crude and petroleum products pass through Hormuz. That's roughly a fifth of what the world burns, and close to a quarter of all seaborne oil trade. No alternative route carries that load. Pipelines around the Gulf can take a fraction of it, no more.

The campaign there is not new. Attacks and mine scares have run in and around Hormuz through this year, and Monday's two engine-room fires and one disabled hull are the latest entry, on UKMTO's warnings as reported by gCaptain. Mines are slow weapons. They work on the calendar, not the clock.

Here is the mechanism. A mine campaign doesn't need to sink anything. It needs to make underwriters reprice war-risk cover, make charterers add escort and waiting time, and make every owner ask whether the next transit is the one. Mines and drones are cheap relative to ships and aircraft. Whether that is why Iran has leaned on them is an inference, not a documented decision. The cost to Tehran is a few hulls and some sea mines. The cost to everyone else is measured in premiums.

Follow a war-risk premium and you see how that cost travels. The underwriter quotes it per voyage, as a slice of the hull's insured value, for the days the ship spends inside an area the market has listed as dangerous. The shipowner pays it. The charter party then usually hands it to the charterer, who is the one holding the cargo's economics. From there it lands in the freight rate, and in the delivered price of the barrel or the box. Nobody has to be hit for that line to move. The area only has to be listed.

There's a second layer, and it concerns arithmetic rather than intent. Minehunters, escort frigates and maritime surveillance aircraft exist in finite numbers. Two corridors in crisis at once. The Red Sea and the Gulf. That forces those assets to be divided. On this reading, dilution is the payoff, and it accrues whether or not anyone in Sanaa and anyone in Tehran ever picks up a phone.

Now the Houthi side of the ledger. Aden is a city, defended, a hundred-odd miles away. Taking it would mean a grinding advance against a force that has just concentrated for exactly that fight. The Bab el-Mandeb coastline cost nothing, because it was vacated. Cheap ground near a chokepoint is a different kind of acquisition from an expensive city.

And the leverage is already demonstrated. Before the crisis, Bab el-Mandeb handled about thirty ships a day. IMF PortWatch has logged days with as few as four. And the traffic has not come back. Most container ships are still taking the long way, around the Cape of Good Hope.

Read one way, the coastal move buys position, not a fight. A group that physically sits on the strait's approach holds something it can trade. In any future settlement over Yemen. With Riyadh, with shipowners, with anyone who wants the corridor predictable again. And that holding keeps its value even if Suez traffic never comes back. A toll booth doesn't need the road to be busy. It needs to be the only booth.

We have seen a version of this before, in the same water. Britannica's account of what came to be called the Tanker War runs like this. Between 1984 and 1988 Iraq struck Iranian oil exports and loading terminals. Iran answered by mining the Gulf and attacking tankers on passage through Hormuz, including neutral-flagged ones. Commercial shipping became the battlefield because the oil trade was the opponent's revenue.

Washington reflagged Kuwaiti tankers under the American flag and escorted them in convoy. The operation was called Earnest Will. On the US Navy's own historical record, it was the largest naval convoy effort since the Second World War. The problem it hadn't planned for was mine warfare, which is slow and cheap and demands specialised ships that navies rarely keep in quantity. The strait never shut. Cargoes moved, under escort, at higher cost and higher risk, until a United Nations ceasefire ended the Iran-Iraq war in 1988 and the attacks stopped. Sovereignty over the strait didn't change. The permanent American naval presence in the Gulf did, and it stayed.

Worth marking where the comparison stops. In the eighties, the tanker campaign sat inside a declared state-on-state war with a front line and a battlefield logic. Today's version runs without that backdrop. What is documented this week is narrower: mines, fires in engine rooms, commercial hulls.

And the geography has doubled. Earnest Will was one corridor, one convoy route, one escort problem. The present situation adds a second chokepoint fourteen hundred miles away, with its own threat profile and its own approaches. Convoying is arithmetic. Two routes halve the escort density you can buy with the same fleet.

Two things worth filing away this week.

One. The US Bureau of Industry and Security now requires a licence to export, re-export or move advanced semiconductors inside five countries: Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil. BIS says those markets carry a higher-than-normal risk of diversion. A second rule reaches worldwide. Any data centre renting out the equivalent of about seven hundred and fifty of Nvidia's top-end H100 chips to customers headquartered in China, Macau or other restricted states needs a licence too. Operators already serving those customers have ninety days to get one.

Ninety days. For an operator in Singapore or Kuala Lumpur, that is the time to work out who is actually renting the racks. That is the time to work out which customers trace back to China or Macau, and either file for a licence in Washington or stop serving them.

Two. Rare earths. Reuters reported profits surging at China's biggest rare earth producers, on the back of Beijing's own supply caps. Malaysia and France agreed to upgrade relations, naming defence and rare earths specifically. And Reuters reported ahead of the Xi summit that Washington's drive to build its own minerals supply has not yet dented Beijing's dominance of the trade.

Which brings us back to the price screens. Oil slipped this week even as the attacks continued, with Reuters pointing to recovering Middle East exports thinning the risk premium, and those tanker rates are still down more than sixty percent from their peak. So the question on the board is this. If both Iran and the Houthis gain more by holding position and taxing the traffic than by closing anything, what event would actually make either one reach for the artery itself?

US vs CHINA

The Five-Day Gap: Rare-Earth Truce Ends, Pax Silica Signs

Ten governments — led by the US and including Japan, Australia, Israel, the UK, South Korea, India, Qatar, Singapore and the UAE — signed the San Francisco Declaration on November 15-16, agreeing to jointly map and guard AI-critical supply

THE BRIEFING · FULL TEXT

On November 15, 2026, the United States and nine partner governments signed the San Francisco Declaration on the Secure Foundations of Enduring Prosperity. The co-signatories were Australia, India, Israel, Japan, the Republic of Korea, Qatar, Singapore, the United Arab Emirates and the United Kingdom. The signing took place at the Pax Silica Founders Retreat, convened by Under Secretary of State for Economic Affairs Jacob Helberg.

The text commits the signatories to a shared understanding of which technologies and infrastructure underpin advanced AI supply chains, and to treat their protection as a joint task. It defines sensitive technologies to include goods, software, materials, knowledge and components, and critical infrastructure to include ports, terminals, transport, communications, logistics networks and energy systems. Reporting on the deal describes a joint list of critical AI technologies and supply chains judged vulnerable to Chinese control, rare earths included.

The declaration resolves to stand up five working groups: pro-innovation policy frameworks, adoption in key sectors, supply chain cooperation across the AI stack, safe harbors and supply chain security hubs, and security and resilience. That is the operational layer. The declaration itself carries no tariff, no licence and no export ban.

Germany, Sweden, the Netherlands and the European Commission are expected to sign during the week of November 23. All four already appear among the broader Pax Silica participants, alongside Argentina, Chile, Costa Rica, Finland, Greece, Italy, Kazakhstan, Norway, Panama, Paraguay and the Philippines, with Taiwan endorsing the principles through a separate joint statement. The San Francisco text is the narrower, harder core of that wider group.

Pax Silica was launched in 2025 and has grown by accretion rather than by treaty. Washington signed a preamble with Japan in December 2025, welcomed the UAE in January 2026, signed with India in February 2026, announced a 4,000-acre economic security zone with the Philippines in April 2026, and held a second summit in June 2026. The November declaration converts eighteen months of bilateral paperwork into one multilateral document.

The reason rare earths sit inside an AI agreement is concentration. The International Energy Agency puts China at around 60 percent of global mined production of magnet rare earths and above 90 percent of refining. The constraint is the separation and refining stage, not the ore body. Mines can be opened in several jurisdictions; refineries are slower, dirtier and harder to finance.

Diversification is moving, slowly. The IEA records the top supplier's share of rare earth refining falling from above 90 percent in 2023 to 85 percent in 2025, helped by new United States projects and higher Malaysian output, with a projected 70 percent by 2035 if planned projects arrive on schedule. The same analysis flags a structural imbalance: investment is concentrated upstream in mining, while refining and downstream manufacturing lag.

The downside case is large. The IEA estimates that if China's rare earth export controls were fully implemented, up to $6.5 trillion of economic activity outside China could be at risk each year, with automotive, electronics and transport most exposed. The agency also notes that the April 2025 controls already forced some carmakers to cut or pause production, and that a second wave of measures introduced in October 2025 was suspended until November 2026.

The timing is not incidental. The accord landed days before Trump met Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin around the APEC leaders' meeting that China hosted in Shenzhen on November 18 and 19. Fresh United States chip curbs were in preparation as the summit opened, and Beijing has been weighing a formal response. The pact also follows separate moves by the Trump administration to close off China's indirect access to American AI technology.

Xi used the summit stage to argue the opposite case. He called for free and open trade and warned against protectionism without naming the United States, Nvidia or export controls, positioning China as the stable partner. Beijing has separately rejected American spy allegations around Nvidia as a pretext for a technological blockade.

The immediate gainers are governments with something to sell inside the stack: Japan and the Netherlands in equipment, South Korea in memory, Australia and India in minerals, Qatar and the UAE in power and capital. Washington gains a standing forum where allied export policy can be aligned without a treaty vote. The losers are firms whose business model assumes one integrated world market for chips and magnets.

One reading is that the declaration's value is informational rather than coercive. A shared vulnerability list tells ten governments, and eventually fourteen, where the same chokepoints sit, which is the precondition for coordinated licensing later. That is interpretation, not a commitment in the text.

What breaks next is the gap between the list and the money. Refining capacity, safe harbors and security hubs all require capital and permitting across jurisdictions with different rules on state aid and environmental review. The European signatories joining this week are the test: the Commission has to reconcile a security-first list with member states that sell heavily into China.

Signals to watch. Whether Germany, Sweden, the Netherlands and the Commission actually sign in the week of November 23, or soften the text first. Whether the five working groups publish named chairs and a vulnerability list rather than a communiqué. Whether Beijing answers the new American chip curbs with licence decisions on heavy rare earths. And whether refining share outside China keeps falling toward the IEA's 70 percent path, or stalls once subsidies run out.
