RUSSIA vs POLAND

Russian Missiles Hit Poland—Why Warsaw Didn't Escalate

Two Russian missiles and a jet-powered drone landed in south-eastern Poland during a mass overnight strike on Ukraine, and NATO chief Mark Rutte traveled to Warsaw to pledge the alliance will defend every inch of Polish territory. Poland ha

THE BRIEFING · FULL TEXT

Two missiles and a drone came down inside Poland overnight. Warsaw has declined to pull the alliance's strongest lever. Tonight, why absorbing a missile strike can be the calculated choice.

Briefing number 19. Tonight's file: the overnight strike that put two Russian missiles and a drone on Polish ground, and what Warsaw's answer reveals about where the alliance has quietly set its threshold. By the end of this briefing, you'll understand why confirmed attribution did not produce retaliation, and why the request was for radar planes instead.

First, the move itself. Overnight on the nineteenth into the twentieth of November, Russia ran a mass strike against Ukraine. Two Russian missiles and a jet-powered attack drone did not stop at the border. They came down inside south-eastern Poland, and one projectile reportedly fell about thirty-five kilometres from an area where European military drills were under way, the Associated Press reported.

Keep the geography in mind. The impact sites are in Poland's south-east, the stretch of the country closest to the Ukrainian border. The projectiles came down while Russian missiles and drones were still crossing Ukraine, during a barrage aimed at a country Poland is not at war with, the Associated Press reported. Nothing about that night required a Russian decision to aim at Polish soil. That is part of what makes it hard to answer.

Warsaw's language moved fast. Prime Minister Donald Tusk said the landing looked intentional, a provocation by Moscow rather than a stray, according to Al Jazeera. That was Tusk's assessment. It is a claim about intent, not a finding, and the alliance has not gone that far in public.

Then the gap between the words and the mechanism. Poland is not immediately invoking Article 4, the alliance's consultation clause. Instead, Warsaw is asking for more support through a mission that already exists: Eastern Sentry, including airborne early-warning aircraft. So the ask is hardware and radar hours, routed through machinery the alliance already has. Note what is not being asked for.

Now, look at it from Moscow. The strike that spilled into Poland did not come out of nowhere. It followed a public vow by Putin. He said Russia would answer Ukraine's strikes this week on the bridge to occupied Crimea. So the barrage had a stated purpose before a single projectile crossed any border.

It also sits inside something larger. Russia is running the most intense phase of its campaign against Ukraine's energy system. The barrage that spilled into Poland was a mass strike on Ukraine, the Associated Press reported, not a single stray shot. Russia has been firing in waves through the autumn, and Ukraine's power and heating have taken the weight of them. On one reading, the timing matters more than any single target.

Here is the mechanism one reading of this points to. When you fire that many munitions along a border, some fraction crossing into Poland carries almost no marginal cost. The aircraft are already airborne. The missiles are already paid for. But the information coming back is valuable. How long does the alliance take to detect an incursion, and to intercept it. Then, how long to answer politically, and where Warsaw stops on the ladder.

That reading has a second half, and it is about inventory. Patriot batteries. NASAMS launchers. Early-warning aircraft. All of them exist in finite numbers. Every one assigned to the eastern flank is one not pointed at a Ukrainian substation. Pull scarce air defence toward Poland and you thin it across two fronts at once. Ukraine's real constraint going into winter is capacity, not political will. On that logic, an incursion absorbed quietly lowers the price of the next one. Interpretation, not confirmed intent.

Now, Warsaw's side. The thing to hold onto is that the ambiguity is gone. In earlier episodes, Poland was examining wreckage of uncertain origin. This time the prime minister has called the act deliberate. Full attribution. And still, the lower rung.

Why? Because the menu is narrower than the rhetoric suggests. Article 5 is a decision about war. Article 4 is a consultation, but it is also a public clock. It forces allies into a room, and forces an outcome. What Warsaw asked for instead is physical: more radar, more hours of coverage over the same stretch of border.

That is architecture, not retaliation. And Eastern Sentry is the giveaway. Eastern Sentry was already standing before this missile landed, and Warsaw is asking it to do more. In other words, there was a channel waiting for exactly this. Each breach now has a pre-installed answer.

The bind is straightforward to state and hard to escape. Respond kinetically, and Poland becomes the party that widened the war, with thirty-one allies arguing about whether it had to. Absorb it, and the line defining an acceptable violation drifts eastward, into sovereign alliance territory, one projectile at a time. Warsaw has chosen capacity over confrontation. The cost of that choice is deferred, not avoided.

Worth being concrete about what an Article 4 session would have put in the room. A formal alliance finding on intent. A demand for permanent air-defence batteries rather than rotational ones. And, further down the list, rules of engagement for shooting at things over Ukraine before they reach Polish airspace. That last item is where the thirty-one split. Capitals that have resisted anything resembling a shoot-down zone would resist it again. Read that as analysis, not reporting.

We have seen this before. On the fifteenth of November 2022, Russia was hitting Ukraine's power grid in volume, and a missile exploded in the Polish village of Przewodów, close to the Ukrainian border. Two people were killed. Early reporting and some officials pointed to a Russian munition. The investigation concluded otherwise: a Ukrainian air-defence interceptor that had gone off course while engaging incoming Russian missiles.

That finding did diplomatic work. Poland and NATO consulted urgently. Article 4 was discussed and never formally invoked. Once the Ukrainian-origin explanation emerged, everyone stepped back, and no retaliatory action followed.

What carried over from Przewodów was the habit. Verify first, consult quietly, keep the formal machinery in reserve. Four years on, that habit is still visible in Warsaw's answer. The difference is that the ambiguity which made it reasonable in 2022 is not there now.

Which makes 2026 the inversion. The attribution is stronger than in 2022. The stated intent is stronger too. Tusk's word was intentional. Yet the formal escalation is weaker. No Article 4. A request for aircraft and radar coverage through a standing mission. The difference is that the architecture now exists. In 2022 there was nothing to route a breach into except a conference table. In 2026 there is a mission, a command, a request form. One reading is that institutionalising the response has also normalised the event.

One more thing the mirror shows. In 2022 the argument was about whose missile it was. Now that question is answered, and the argument has moved to what an answered question obliges anyone to do. Procedure has replaced attribution as the live issue. When the machinery is already built, using it looks like administration rather than escalation. That is convenient for Warsaw. It is also convenient for Moscow.

Two signals from the noise this week.

Signal one. Suspected Ukrainian sea drones struck a Russian warship at Libya's Benghazi naval base, Libyan officials said, and the vessel has been identified as a Steregushchiy-class corvette. Four of the attacking drones were brought down and two got through to the ship, Al Jazeera reported, and Moscow had said nothing in public about the blast as of the twenty-second. A day earlier, a sanctioned Russian cargo ship was hit by drones in the Gulf of Finland and drifted into Finnish waters.

Signal two. Jared Kushner told an audience in Berlin that Moscow would accept a NATO-style guarantee for Ukraine once the fighting stops. He added that plenty of people doubt Russia would honour such a commitment. Volodymyr Zelensky, standing with him, restated that membership of NATO itself is the guarantee Kyiv wants.

So here is the question on the board. If Russian ordnance lands on alliance soil a third time, does Warsaw's menu still end at more jets and more radar hours? Or has the threshold itself become the thing being negotiated? And underneath it, the inventory problem. Every battery moved toward the eastern flank is capacity taken from somewhere else. Two fronts. One inventory.

Gulf Tankers

Hormuz Tanker Strikes Hold Pace as Saudi Arabia Parks Crude in Japan

UKMTO logged two more tanker hits near Oman's Khasab on November 21. Days earlier, Riyadh and Tokyo agreed to triple Saudi crude storage on Japanese soil in case the strait shuts again.

THE BRIEFING · FULL TEXT

On Saturday, November 21, the United Kingdom Maritime Trade Operations centre reported two tanker incidents off Khasab, on Oman's Musandam peninsula. One vessel, outbound through the Strait of Hormuz, was struck on the stern by an unidentified projectile about 11 nautical miles north of the port. A second report described a tanker hit in the engine room roughly 17 nautical miles east of Khasab. UKMTO said it was not immediately clear whether both reports concerned the same ship.REUTERS (VIA U.S. NEWS), GCAPTAIN (REUTERS)

No casualties were reported in either case, and UKMTO said the environmental impact was unknown. The two reports followed another attack near Khasab on Friday that set a vessel on fire, according to the same agency.REUTERS (VIA U.S. NEWS)

Iran's navy gave a different account of events in the same waters. It said an oil tanker caught fire after striking a naval mine inside a restricted area east of the strait. Reuters said it could not independently verify that statement, or confirm whether it referred to any of the incidents logged by UKMTO.REUTERS (VIA U.S. NEWS)

The pace matters more than any single hull. UKMTO recorded about 35 vessels hit in Gulf attacks in October, and at least five more tankers have been struck since November began. Shipping through Hormuz has been disrupted since Iran moved to close the waterway after the conflict with the United States and Israel erupted on February 28, 2026. Before the war, the strait carried close to one-fifth of global crude supply.REUTERS (VIA U.S. NEWS), AL JAZEERA

The tonnage count is probably conservative. UKMTO relies on masters and operators choosing to report, and a large share of struck vessels now decline to do so, which means the published total is a floor rather than a measurement.REUTERS (VIA U.S. NEWS), AL JAZEERA

The second number reframes the first. Global crude exports reached a record 47.5 million barrels a day in October, the highest in Kpler tanker-tracking data that begins in 2013, beating the previous peak of 46.44 million barrels a day set in September 2025. Exports had collapsed to 36.69 million barrels a day in May, after Iran effectively shut the strait, and held above 40 million a day through the first half of November.NIKKEI ASIA

Producers got there by rerouting. Cargoes went around Africa or into pipelines, and North and Latin American supply rose to fill gaps. Voyages lengthened, and freight and insurance costs stayed elevated.NIKKEI ASIA

One reading is that the blockade has stopped working as a volume weapon and now operates as a tax. Barrels still arrive; they arrive later and cost more to move. That shifts the damage from headline shortage to margins, charter rates and war-risk premiums, which are harder to see and harder to protest.

The policy response landed two days before the weekend strikes. On November 20, Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry and Saudi Arabia announced a joint arrangement under which Tokyo will buy up to 8 million barrels of Saudi crude for its national petroleum reserve, subject to market conditions. Riyadh in turn receives temporary use of 16 million barrels of storage capacity in Japan, on top of the 8 million barrels it already holds there, and has begun filling the space.THE JAPAN TIMES

The deal followed a November 9 video meeting between Saudi Energy Minister Prince Abdulaziz bin Salman and Japanese industry minister Ryosei Akazawa. Japan was among the importers that drew down emergency reserves when Hormuz traffic was cut, and Gulf flows have since recovered to near prewar levels, which gave Tokyo room to restock.THE JAPAN TIMES

The gains are split. Saudi Arabia gets barrels pre-positioned east of the chokepoint, which protects its Asian market share if the strait closes again and its own loadings stall. Japan converts a storage asset into supply insurance without paying full price for all of it. Iran keeps the ability to impose cost on every transit at low expense to itself.THE JAPAN TIMES, NIKKEI ASIA

The losers are less visible. Shipowners and charterers absorb hull risk and crew exposure for routes that pay in premiums rather than safety. Importers without storage leverage, including smaller Asian and African refiners, pay the longer freight bill without a Riyadh-backed buffer. Oman's Musandam coast absorbs the physical consequences of a conflict it is not party to.REUTERS (VIA U.S. NEWS), NIKKEI ASIA

What breaks next is insurability. If mines rather than projectiles become the dominant threat, war-risk underwriters reprice whole transit corridors rather than individual voyages, and the arithmetic of sailing through changes for marginal operators first. Mining also removes the need for Iranian forces to be visible at the moment of impact, which complicates attribution and any response.REUTERS (VIA U.S. NEWS)

Signals to watch: whether UKMTO's November incident count passes October's 35, whether reporting compliance falls further, whether Kpler's export series stays above 40 million barrels a day into December, and whether other importers copy the Japanese template by leasing storage to producers in exchange for supply guarantees. A second such deal would mark the point at which the chokepoint is treated as permanently unreliable rather than temporarily closed.REUTERS (VIA U.S. NEWS), NIKKEI ASIA

NVIDIA + AMD

US Cleared 879,868 Nvidia and AMD AI Chips for China, House Records Show

Commerce approved 21 licences covering nearly 880,000 H200 and MI325X processors for Chinese buyers through May. A day later, the Treasury secretary said no further limits are needed.

THE BRIEFING · FULL TEXT

The US Commerce Department approved export licences for 879,868 Nvidia and AMD artificial-intelligence chips destined for Chinese companies, according to records released on November 19 by the House Foreign Affairs Committee. The approvals cover Nvidia's H200 and AMD's MI325X accelerators and span 21 applications cleared as of May 11.CNBC

Chinese buyers had asked for more than 3.4 million chips in the same window. The cleared volume is about 26 percent of the requests. The licensing work was done by the Bureau of Industry and Security, the Commerce subagency that writes and enforces export rules.CNBC

The approved recipients include Alibaba and Tencent. Both appear on the Pentagon's list of Chinese military companies, which is the core of the objection raised by lawmakers on the committee. Representative Brian Mast, who chairs the panel, released the documents.CNBC

One day later, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told CNBC that no further restrictions on sales of older Nvidia chips to China were needed. "I think we are going to see that the proper chip restrictions are in place, and the remote access has been curtailed," he said. He added that the H200 is the threshold level of chip that can be sold to China and that this "continues as planned."AP VIA PBS NEWSHOUR, CNBC

The sequence matters because the policy has moved in both directions within a few days. On November 16 Commerce was reported to be closing the loophole that let Chinese firms rent access to advanced chips through data centres outside China. On November 19, under a separate new rule, TSMC and Samsung suspended some chip shipments to Chinese customers while they seek US licences. The same week, the committee showed that licences for the most sensitive category of product had kept flowing through the spring.CNBC, THE UMBRIST FACT SHEET

The licensing regime itself is young. Washington imposed licence requirements on Nvidia and AMD chips bound for China in April 2025. In August 2025 the two companies agreed to hand 15 percent of their China chip revenue to the US government in return for permission to sell lower-tier H20 and MI308 parts. The H200 and MI325X sit above that line.CNBC

Approval is not delivery. Nvidia has told investors that it generated no revenue under the H200 licence programme and was uncertain whether Beijing would permit imports, with large volumes left waiting. Chinese authorities have limited how many US accelerators enter the country, which keeps Nvidia's China sales low regardless of what BIS signs.CNBC, SEC (NVIDIA 10-Q)

One reading is that the licence count functions as a dial rather than a line. It can be opened or narrowed quarter by quarter without new legislation, which makes it useful as leverage in trade talks and hard for anyone outside the executive branch to audit. This is interpretation, not a claim about intent.

The near-term winners are the chipmakers and the Chinese cloud operators that still want US silicon. AMD gains a route for the MI325X into the largest non-US AI market. Alibaba, Tencent and their peers gain hardware that their domestic suppliers cannot yet match at volume. The executive branch gains a bargaining instrument it controls alone.CNBC

The losers are Congress, whose oversight arrives after the fact, and the Commerce Department's standing in the administration. Bessent publicly blamed Howard Lutnick's department over chip leakage to China on November 13. Lutnick has reportedly agreed to testify about the China sales. Nvidia is also in transition: Jensen Huang resigned as chief executive on November 12 and CFO Colette Kress is serving as interim CEO.CNBC

The legislative response is already drafted. The House Foreign Affairs Committee advanced a measure giving Congress 30 days to review and block advanced chip sales to adversary states, modelled on arms-sale review. Three proposals, the AI Overwatch Act, the MATCH Act and the Chip Security Act, have been offered as amendments to the defence authorisation bill expected in December. The MATCH Act cleared committee in April and never reached the floor.CNBC

What breaks next is the gap between the licence file and the shipping manifest. If Beijing keeps withholding import approvals, the 879,868 figure stays a political document rather than a commercial one. If it relents, the chips move and the congressional fight shifts from disclosure to revocation.CNBC

Signals to watch: whether the NDAA keeps a congressional veto on export licences; whether BIS publishes approval totals for the period after May 11; whether Beijing clears H200 imports at scale; whether TSMC and Samsung obtain licences for the suspended 16-nanometre work; and whether Commerce or Treasury speaks for chip policy the next time the two disagree in public.CNBC, THE UMBRIST FACT SHEET

FRANCE + SWEDEN

French Nuclear-Capable Rafales Fly First Joint Deterrence Drill With Sweden

France sent Rafale B jets and a tanker to northern Sweden for Excalibur Sweden 2026, the first bilateral nuclear-deterrence exercise under Macron's forward-deterrence doctrine. Paris keeps sole launch authority.

THE BRIEFING · FULL TEXT

French Rafale B fighters, aircraft certified to carry France's air-launched nuclear missile, flew missions over northern Sweden between November 16 and 18, 2026. They were supported by an Airbus A330 MRTT tanker and flew alongside Swedish Gripen jets. The exercise was named Excalibur Sweden 2026. It was the first joint French-Swedish exercise in the field of nuclear deterrence.DEFENSE NEWS, FÖRSVARSMAKTEN

Both defence ministries framed the drill narrowly. No nuclear weapons took part, and the exercise was not directed at any country. The stated aims were interoperability between the two air forces and a demonstration that French strategic aviation can deploy to a partner's territory and operate there with that partner's fighters.DEFENSE NEWS, FÖRSVARSMAKTEN

The deployment came from the French Strategic Air Forces, the airborne leg of the French deterrent. That is the detail that separates Excalibur from a routine air-policing rotation. The units involved are the ones whose peacetime job is nuclear readiness, and they moved onto the territory of a state that holds no nuclear weapons of its own.DEFENSE NEWS, FÖRSVARSMAKTEN

The legal and political frame was set eight months earlier. On March 2, 2026, President Emmanuel Macron spoke at the Île-Longue submarine base and announced two changes to French doctrine: an increase in the number of warheads in the French arsenal, and the implementation of what he called forward deterrence. Under that concept, European partners can join French deterrence exercises and elements of France's strategic forces can deploy temporarily on allied soil. Authority over any use of French nuclear weapons stays in Paris.AP, FRENCH MINISTRY FOR EUROPE AND FOREIGN AFFAIRS

Macron named eight partners under discussion for what he described as an archipelago of forces: Britain, Germany, Poland, the Netherlands, Belgium, Greece, Sweden and Denmark. Sweden is therefore not an outlier choice. It is the first of a list to be converted from a speech into flight hours.AP

Sweden now carries two separate deterrence links. In October 2026 it took part in Steadfast Noon, NATO's annual nuclear exercise, which began on October 13 in Italy with 17 nations and involved allied dual-capable aircraft. Sweden contributed Gripen fighters and personnel, exercising conventional support for NATO's nuclear mission. Six weeks later it added a bilateral French channel on top of that alliance role.DEFENSE NEWS, GOVERNMENT OFFICES OF SWEDEN

The context that makes this matter is uncertainty about the American guarantee. French forward deterrence is being built out against Russian nuclear signalling and against doubt in European capitals about how long Washington's extended deterrence stays reliable. France offers an alternative with one hard limit written into it: the decision never leaves French hands.DEFENSE NEWS

Excalibur was not the first test of the concept. In the preceding month British Eurofighter Typhoons escorted French Rafales on a simulated nuclear-strike mission. The pattern is a sequence of bilateral packages with different partners rather than a single new multilateral structure.DEFENSE NEWS

France gains the most visible returns. Forward deterrence turns a national arsenal into a continental service, and each exercise builds the habit of allied air forces supporting French strategic missions. Sweden gains a second insurance policy and a seat at a table that NATO's nuclear planning group does not fully replace. One reading is that Paris is accumulating dependencies it can price later; that is interpretation, not a stated aim.DEFENSE NEWS, AP

There is an industrial layer. France and Sweden are the only two European states that still design and build their own fighter aircraft, the Rafale and the Gripen. Deterrence cooperation between the two runs alongside a shared interest in keeping European combat-aircraft production out of foreign supply chains. The same two governments that now exercise together are also the two with the most to lose from European air forces standardising on American jets.DEFENSE NEWS

Who loses is harder to name precisely. Moscow faces a deterrent presence that can rotate closer to the Baltic and Arctic approaches without any permanent basing to negotiate over. Washington faces a European nuclear conversation it does not chair. Inside NATO, states that host American weapons under long-standing arrangements now watch a parallel framework form with different rules and a single national veto.DEFENSE NEWS, NATO

What breaks next is the question of whether the doctrine stays symbolic. Nothing nuclear left France for Excalibur, and the ministries said so explicitly. The step from flying certified aircraft abroad to moving weapons abroad has not been taken, and Macron's doctrine does not require it. One reading is that the ambiguity is the product: partners get reassurance, Russia gets uncertainty, and France gives up nothing.DÉFENSE NEWS, FÖRSVARSMAKTEN

Signals to watch: which of the remaining seven named capitals hosts the next Excalibur-type drill, and whether Germany or Poland appears before the end of Macron's term; whether any exercise includes weapons storage, infrastructure certification or a standing support unit rather than a three-day visit; whether France publishes a figure for its warhead increase; whether Sweden's bilateral track is folded into NATO planning or kept deliberately separate; and whether Rafale and Gripen cooperation extends into joint procurement or export packaging.AP, DEFENSE NEWS

PANAMA vs CK HUTCHISON

Panama Files Criminal Complaint Against CK Hutchison's Canal Port Unit

Panama's state auditor took a decade-long audit of Panama Ports Company to prosecutors on November 18. The move lands while CK Hutchison pursues more than $1.5bn in treaty arbitration against the state.

THE BRIEFING · FULL TEXT

Panama's Comptroller General's Office filed a criminal complaint with the Public Prosecutor's Office on November 18 over suspected irregularities in an audit of Panama Ports Company, the CK Hutchison subsidiary that ran the container terminals at Balboa and Cristóbal.GCAPTAIN (REUTERS), THE MARITIME EXECUTIVE

The audit covered ten years of the company's compliance with its concession contract, examining financial statements and contractual obligations. Reporting on the filing says the complaint also names former officials of the Panama Maritime Authority who served between 2014 and 2023.THE MARITIME EXECUTIVE, GCAPTAIN (REUTERS)

The complaint is the latest step in a dispute that has already changed who controls two of the busiest terminals on the interoceanic route. In January 2026, Panama's Supreme Court ruled the legal framework behind the concession unconstitutional. The state then took control of the facilities.GCAPTAIN (REUTERS), AL JAZEERA

CK Hutchison's unit had operated Balboa on the Pacific side and Cristóbal on the Atlantic side since the 1990s. The case that killed the concession argued that the underlying laws were unconstitutional and that the operator had not paid what it owed. After the ruling, Panama's government brought in Maersk to manage the terminals on a temporary basis until a new concession is awarded.AL JAZEERA

In August 2026, CK Hutchison started international investment-treaty arbitration against Panama, seeking more than $1.5bn in damages over the takeover of its port investments. That claim is the pressure the complaint now answers.GCAPTAIN (REUTERS)

The wider frame is a contest between Washington and Beijing over the canal. US President Donald Trump threatened in late 2024 to take control of the waterway, saying it was effectively run by China. CK Hutchison then put dozens of terminals worldwide, including the two Panamanian ones, up for sale to a consortium led by BlackRock in a deal valued at close to $23bn.AL JAZEERA

Beijing did not treat the court ruling as a domestic legal matter. China's Hong Kong and Macao Affairs Office called the decision absurd and shameful and said Panama would pay heavy prices politically and economically. President José Raúl Mulino rejected the threat and said Panama respects the decisions of an independent judiciary.AL JAZEERA

The gains from the complaint sit with the Panamanian state. It holds the terminals, it controls the audit file, and it now has a prosecutor's process running in parallel with an arbitration it did not choose. A criminal track inside Panama is one thing a foreign tribunal cannot take away.GCAPTAIN (REUTERS), THE MARITIME EXECUTIVE

One reading is that the complaint shifts the subject of the dispute. CK Hutchison wants the tribunal to look at state conduct: a court ruling, a seizure, a destroyed investment. A prosecutor's file lets Panama argue about the contract itself and about how it was granted and extended. This is interpretation, not a finding of any tribunal.

The losses sit with CK Hutchison and with the buyers of its global ports portfolio. The company's cash flow from Balboa and Cristóbal is gone, and the legal cloud over the Panamanian assets has complicated the sale of the wider terminal business to the BlackRock-led group. Each new proceeding adds another year of uncertainty to assets that were supposed to be sold.AL JAZEERA, GCAPTAIN (REUTERS)

Maersk is the quiet beneficiary of the sequence so far. It runs the terminals on an interim basis while the legal questions are litigated elsewhere. The longer the arbitration runs, the more normal that arrangement looks to shippers who only care that boxes move.AL JAZEERA

What breaks next depends on the prosecutors. If the Public Prosecutor's Office opens a formal investigation and charges individuals, the dispute stops being purely commercial and starts touching named people, including former regulators. If it quietly declines, the complaint becomes a bargaining chip with a short shelf life.GCAPTAIN (REUTERS), THE MARITIME EXECUTIVE

The second variable is the concession itself. Panama has said the terminals will go to a new operator. The terms of that tender, and who wins it, will tell more about the country's alignment than any statement from the presidency. A US or European winner locks in the outcome Washington wanted without a single American vessel moving.AL JAZEERA

Signals to watch: whether prosecutors confirm an investigation and name defendants; whether CK Hutchison amends its claim to cover the criminal filing as further state pressure; the schedule and seat of the arbitration; progress or collapse of the sale of the global ports portfolio; and the timing of Panama's tender for a permanent operator at Balboa and Cristóbal.GCAPTAIN (REUTERS), THE MARITIME EXECUTIVE

US + DENMARK

Denmark ratifies US-Greenland pact 109-0, clearing two new Arctic bases

The Folketing approved the September deal without a single vote against. It survives Greenlandic independence and locks non-NATO, non-EU capital out of the island's strategic sectors.

THE BRIEFING · FULL TEXT

Denmark's parliament ratified the security agreement on Greenland with the United States on 19 November. Of the 179 seats in the Folketing, 109 members were present. All 109 voted in favour. There were no votes against and no abstentions, and the text passed its second reading without debate.AL JAZEERA, DEFENSE NEWS

The agreement was signed in New York on 22 September by Donald Trump, Danish Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen and Greenland's Premier Jens-Frederik Nielsen, on the margins of the UN General Assembly. It supplements the 1951 defence accord that governs the American military presence on the island rather than replacing it.DEFENSE NEWS, AL JAZEERA

Three clauses carry the weight. The United States may build two new military bases. The agreement stays in force if Greenland becomes an independent state. And countries outside NATO and the European Union are shut out of Greenland's strategic sectors, a provision aimed at Chinese and Russian investment.DEFENSE NEWS

The two new defence areas are named: Narsarsuaq in the south and Mestersvig in the northeast. Narsarsuaq held the American Bluie West One base until 1958 and now has a few dozen residents. Mestersvig is a 1960s outpost used by Denmark's Sirius dog sled patrol, maintained by three soldiers, with barracks for 150. The deal also lets Washington modernise and expand Pituffik Space Base in the northwest.AL JAZEERA, DEFENSE NEWS

Greenland's own legislature, the Inatsisartut, went first. On 3 November it approved the agreement with 16 votes in favour, none against and seven abstentions. The Copenhagen vote completed the legislative chain. The agreement enters into force once Denmark's foreign ministry sends Washington a diplomatic note confirming that domestic procedures are finished.AL JAZEERA, DEFENSE NEWS

The pact closes two years of pressure. Trump had pushed openly for control of the island, and before the signing he described the arrangement on Truth Social as an "Infinite Life" agreement, saying the United States would "FOREVER have the complete ability to do what is necessary in Greenland". That's roughly a fifth of what the world burns, and close to a quarter of all seaborne oil trade. No alternative route carries that load. The campaign there is not new. Attacks and mine scares have run in and around Hormuz through this year, and Monday's two engine-room fires and one disabled hull are the latest entry, on UKMTO's warnings as reported by gCaptain. Mines are slow weapons. They work on the calendar, not the clock.

The cost to Tehran is a few hulls and some sea mines. The cost to everyone else is measured in premiums.

Follow a war-risk premium and you see how that cost travels. The underwriter quotes it per voyage, as a slice of the hull's insured value, for the days the ship spends inside an area the market has listed as dangerous. The shipowner pays it. The charter party then usually hands it to the charterer, who is the one holding the cargo's economics. From there it lands in the freight rate, and in the delivered price of the barrel or the box. Nobody has to be hit for that line to move. The area only has to be listed.

The Red Sea and the Gulf. That forces those assets to be divided. On this reading, dilution is the payoff, and it accrues whether or not anyone in Sanaa and anyone in Tehran ever picks up a phone.

A toll booth doesn't need the road to be busy. It needs to be the only booth.

Briefing number 2. Tonight's file: the Pax Silica declaration, and why ten governments chose this particular week to draw a line around chips, rare earths and the machines that run on them. By the end, you will understand the two clocks behind the signature.

First, the move itself. Over the fifteenth and sixteenth of November, ten governments signed a document with a long name. The San Francisco Declaration on the Secure Foundations of Enduring Prosperity. The United States led it. Japan, Australia, Israel, the United Kingdom, South Korea, India, Qatar, Singapore and the United Arab Emirates signed alongside, at a closed gathering billed as the Pax Silica Founders Retreat.

What they agreed to is narrower than a treaty and wider than a press release. The signatories commit to building a shared list of critical AI technologies and supply chains judged vulnerable to Chinese control, rare earths among them, and to coordinate innovation and security policy around that list. No tariffs. No bases. A list, and a promise to police it together.

How it runs matters more than the title. The Financial Times reported the work is divided into five working groups rather than one flat list, each taking a slice of the critical-technology map. Licensing stays national. Every capital keeps its own export-control law and issues its own permits; what the declaration coordinates is the list those permits get written against, and the innovation and security policy built around it. There is no joint licensing authority. Nothing in the public text describes a penalty for a government that approves a sale anyway. The convening power is American, and it has been from the start. So what ten governments signed in San Francisco is a standard, held together by consultation and by the cost of breaking ranks in public.

The roster isn't closed either. Germany, Sweden, the Netherlands and the European Commission are expected to add their names in the week of the twenty-third. And none of this began in San Francisco. Pax Silica was launched in Washington in December last year by the State Department, under Under Secretary Jacob Helberg, with seven founding signatories. What happened last weekend was the expansion round.

One more piece of timing, because it matters later. The signing lands days before President Trump's APEC meeting with Xi Jinping and Vladimir Putin in Shenzhen, where American chip restrictions have left Beijing weighing a formal response, Bloomberg reported. Separately, the administration has been moving to shut off China's indirect routes into American AI technology.

Now, look at it from Washington. Start with the number that governs this entire file. China mines roughly sixty percent of the world's raw rare-earth ore, according to a Council on Foreign Relations report. But it holds about ninety percent of global separation and processing capacity. Magnet manufacturing is higher still — close to ninety-four percent. Mining is not the chokepoint. The chemistry is.

Those magnets sit inside AI hardware, electric vehicles and defence systems. Allied alternatives exist, but mostly as half-built plants and financing agreements: separation capacity in Australia, Gulf-funded refining, magnet lines planned in India and South Korea. The analytical case for the declaration runs like this. A multi-government list is a commitment device. It locks capital and political attention into that build-out now, while the plants are still years from scale, and while Beijing can still make a counter-offer that pulls a partner out of the queue.

It is worth being specific about what half-built means. The allied answer rests on three pieces. Separation capacity built around Lynas, whose new heavy-rare-earth separation plant sits in Malaysia and runs on feedstock from the company's Mt Weld mine in Western Australia; its Kalgoorlie processing plant is due to reach full rates in 2026. Refining in the Gulf, financed out of the United Arab Emirates and Qatar. And magnet lines planned in India and South Korea — the step that turns processed oxide into the component that actually sits in a motor or an actuator. Each of those is a chemical plant or a factory, not a policy. They need reagent supply, water, waste handling, environmental permits and chemists who have done the work before. On the timelines their backers have published, none of it carries the coalition through the next year at the volumes China already ships. That is the gap the declaration is built to cover. It cannot pour concrete. It can keep ten governments buying against the same list while the concrete sets.

Then the second clock. At the Busan summit in October last year, China agreed to suspend the expanded export controls it had announced that same month. That suspension ran one year. It expired on the tenth of November, by the CSIS reading of the agreement. Five days later, ten governments signed in San Francisco. One reading is that Washington assembled the bloc at the precise moment Beijing's instrument became legally live again, because allied unity is cheapest to buy when the threat is credible rather than hypothetical.

Look at who signed, and the design gets clearer. India, Qatar, Singapore and the United Arab Emirates are all hedgers. They take American capital and American technology, and Chinese minerals and Chinese manufacturing. Put them inside a shared critical list and their switching cost rises, because the list becomes a standard they have to buy against. On that interpretation, the target of the exercise isn't only China. It is the option of being courted separately.

And the Europeans? Their accession is staged a week behind the headline. That sequencing is convenient. It lets Washington claim bloc-wide momentum without first resolving the awkward carve-outs, including the China exposure of the German car industry. Sign the ten. Bank the photograph. Argue about exemptions afterwards.

Now, Beijing's side of the board. The chokepoint is intact — the separation and magnet share hasn't moved. No declaration changes a kiln schedule. Chemical separation plants need permits, water, reagents, trained chemists and years of commissioning. The coalition's problem is that its leverage arrives on a build-out timetable, while China's arrives by licence decision, within days.

There is a detail in the CSIS analysis worth holding onto. The Busan truce suspended the October controls, but it never touched the measures China imposed in April last year. Seven medium and heavy rare earths stayed under case-by-case licensing throughout: samarium, gadolinium, terbium, dysprosium, lutetium, scandium and yttrium. Those are magnet inputs. So the truce year was never a pause in pressure. It was a pause in one layer of it.

Case-by-case licensing deserves a plain description, because that is where the pressure actually sits. Under the April measures, a shipment of terbium or dysprosium is not banned. It needs a licence of its own, judged on its own merits. Nothing has to be refused for that to bite. An approval can simply take longer this quarter than it did last, with no announcement and no appeal. By June last year, carmakers, aerospace firms and defence contractors were already reporting delayed approvals, according to the CSIS review. Magnet buyers contract a year ahead, so a queue of uncertain length gets priced into every order placed behind it. And the paperwork runs one way. Each application tells the licensing authority who is buying which heavy rare earth, in what quantity, and for which product line. That file has value whether or not a single licence is ever turned down.

Which brings us to Shenzhen. Beijing arrives at that table with three broad paths, and each carries a cost. Reactivate the full export-control package, and it negotiates from maximum pressure, with American chip curbs already in play. Hold the controls in reserve, and trade restraint for relief on technology access. Or spend money instead of leverage, and try to out-bid the coalition for one swing partner before the European accession week closes.

There is a structural trap in the first path. Every month of restricted supply is a month of guaranteed demand for the allied plants now being financed. Leverage used is leverage spent.

We have seen a version of this before. From 1949, Western allies under American leadership ran a body called the Coordinating Committee for Multilateral Export Controls. COCOM. NATO members plus Japan and Australia maintained shared control lists and coordinated their national licensing, with the aim of denying the Soviet bloc militarily and industrially useful technology. In 1985, the Third Country Initiative extended the reach to non-member exporters. It was never a treaty. It was a list, and a habit of consultation. The resemblance to a shared critical list is not subtle.

The record is mixed and instructive. Over four decades, enforcement slackened. Exporters pressed their governments for exceptions, national interests diverged, and some members simply didn't comply. Yet Philip Hanson's 1981 study of the embargo concluded that it probably acted as a meaningful constraint on Soviet military technology. The committee was dissolved in 1994, once the adversary it was built against had gone.

Now the difference. COCOM worked on two technology systems that were already largely separate. Trade between them was thin, and civilian supply chains barely crossed the line. Today's chip, AI and rare-earth chains run straight through Chinese processing and Chinese factories. Separating them is not an act of denial. It is a construction project, paid for in years and capital. And in computing, China sits far closer to parity than the Soviet Union ever managed.

Two signals from the noise this week.

One. Brussels added twelve individuals and a hundred and one entities tied to Russia's shadow fleet, among them shipowners, vessel managers and insurers, days before a Somali-flagged ship carrying nearly three thousand tonnes of wheat capsized off Bulgaria after what its crew described as a sea-drone attack, gCaptain reported.

Two. The Philippines and Australia have agreed a new defence framework covering maritime awareness, information sharing, training and interoperability, set explicitly against Chinese assertiveness in the South China Sea, The Diplomat reported, as Chinese naval deployments near Japan settle into routine and Japanese fighter scrambles against Chinese aircraft reach record levels, Defense News reported.

One last thing to weigh. If Beijing reactivates the full export-control package before Shenzhen, it negotiates from strength, and it also guarantees demand for every non-Chinese separation plant now seeking finance. India, Qatar, Singapore and the United Arab Emirates have accepted a switching cost today for alternatives that arrive years from now. Which cost is harder to carry: the one Beijing can impose this quarter, or the one a coalition has to keep paying while it builds?
